From Data to Decisions

Athlete Profiling Through Performance Quadrant Analysis

How data visualization can help bridge the gap between sport science and individualized training practice.

Author: Assist. Prof. Armin Paravlić, PhD

Date: Saturday, October 31, 2026

Introduction: We Collect More Data Than Ever, But Are We Using It Effectively?

Modern sport science has made athlete monitoring more accessible and comprehensive than ever before.

Force plates provide detailed information about jumping performance and neuromuscular function. Dynamometers quantify muscular strength. Tensiomyography allows us to assess muscle contractile properties. Sprint testing provides information about acceleration and maximal running speed, while body composition assessments offer additional insights into an athlete's physical characteristics.

The amount of information available to coaches, strength and conditioning specialists, and sport scientists continues to increase. However, collecting data and making informed decisions are two different things.

A practitioner may receive a report containing dozens of variables, including countermovement jump height, peak force, reactive strength index, isokinetic strength, sprint velocity, and muscle contraction time. Each measurement may be scientifically meaningful. Yet, when presented independently, these variables do not necessarily provide a clear picture of an athlete's overall physical profile.

More importantly, they may not immediately answer the question that matters most in practice: Which physical qualities should we prioritize for this particular athlete?

This is where athlete profiling and effective data visualization become particularly valuable. Rather than treating performance variables as isolated numbers, we can examine how they interact and identify athletes who demonstrate different combinations of physical characteristics.

One practical approach is the Athlete Performance Quadrant, a two-dimensional visualization that places athletes into four distinct performance profiles based on two selected variables. The objective is not to replace scientific analysis or coaching expertise. It is to make performance information more accessible, interpretable, and actionable.

1. What Is Athlete Performance Quadrant Analysis?

A performance quadrant is a scatterplot that simultaneously displays two variables for each athlete. For example, we may be interested in comparing countermovement jump (CMJ) height with maximal sprint velocity, knee extensor strength, or muscle contraction time.

Each athlete is represented by a single point, while two reference lines divide the graph into four quadrants. These boundaries can be determined using the group mean, median, established reference values, or individually defined performance targets.

For variables where higher values indicate better performance, the quadrants can be interpreted as follows:

  • High performers: Athletes above the reference values for both variables.
  • X-dominant athletes: Athletes performing relatively well in the horizontal-axis variable but below the reference for the vertical-axis variable.
  • Y-dominant athletes: Athletes performing relatively well in the vertical-axis variable but below the reference for the horizontal-axis variable.
  • Development priority: Athletes below the reference values for both variables.

An important consideration is that these classifications must account for the direction of each variable. Greater jump height and maximal running speed are generally desirable, whereas lower body fat percentage or shorter muscle contraction time may be favorable in certain performance contexts. The physiological meaning of the variables determines how the quadrants should be interpreted.

2. Translating Published Research Into Practical Athlete Profiles

To demonstrate the application of quadrant analysis, I used performance data from our previously published investigation: The association between unilateral and bilateral performance-related measures in elite female soccer players: A multifaceted investigation (Paravlic et al., 2024).

The study included 35 elite female football players who underwent comprehensive assessments of neuromuscular function, muscular strength, jumping ability, sprint performance, and muscle contractile properties.

Our original investigation demonstrated that relationships between different performance tests were statistically significant in several cases, but their magnitudes and consistency varied considerably. For example, bilateral CMJ height was positively associated with maximal running speed over 30 m (r = 0.649). Conversely, several TMG-derived contraction time variables showed negative associations with jumping and sprinting performance.

No single physical performance test can fully describe an athlete's neuromuscular capabilities.

The quadrant graphs presented below offer an alternative way to explore these data. Unlike conventional correlation tables, they allow us to identify individual athletes who may demonstrate similar outcomes through different combinations of physical characteristics. These quadrant classifications are a secondary visualization of the data, not a validated diagnostic model or a direct result of the original study's statistical analyses.

3. CMJ Height and Biceps Femoris Contraction Time: Exploring Neuromuscular Characteristics

The first quadrant compares CMJ height with the contraction time (Tc) of the biceps femoris muscle, assessed using tensiomyography (TMG). TMG provides information about the mechanical response of skeletal muscle following an electrically induced contraction. Contraction time represents the time required for muscle displacement to increase from 10% to 90% of its maximal amplitude.

In the context of explosive athletic performance, shorter contraction times may be associated with more favorable rapid-contractile characteristics, although their interpretation depends on the muscle, testing protocol, and population.

In our original study, biceps femoris contraction time demonstrated negative associations with several performance variables. Dominant-leg BF Tc correlated with bilateral CMJ height (r = −0.526), while non-dominant-leg BF Tc correlated with maximal 30 m running speed (r = −0.530).

What does the quadrant tell us?

Athletes in the lower-right quadrant combine greater jump height with shorter contraction time. This represents a favorable combination of the two selected characteristics. In contrast, athletes in the upper-left quadrant demonstrate lower jump heights and longer contraction times relative to the sample reference values.

However, the most interesting athletes may be those with contrasting profiles. An athlete may jump relatively high despite having a longer BF contraction time. Another may demonstrate a short BF contraction time but comparatively modest jump performance. These observations suggest that muscle contractile properties and functional performance are related but not interchangeable.

Practical application

A player with low CMJ height and prolonged BF contraction time may warrant further investigation of lower-body strength, explosive force production, and neuromuscular function. However, it would be inappropriate to prescribe a specific hamstring intervention solely because an athlete appears in an unfavorable quadrant. TMG-derived contraction time is not a direct measure of voluntary hamstring strength, nor does a longer Tc automatically indicate a greater injury risk. The quadrant should serve as a starting point for further assessment rather than a definitive diagnosis.

4. CMJ Height and Knee Extensor Strength: Strength Does Not Always Translate Into Jumping Performance

The second quadrant examines CMJ height in relation to knee extensor strength, expressed as peak torque normalized to body mass (Nm/kg). The strength measure represents knee extension peak torque assessed at an angular velocity of 60°/s using isokinetic dynamometry.

Although both tests provide information about lower-body neuromuscular function, they assess different physical capabilities. Isokinetic dynamometry measures joint torque under controlled movement conditions, whereas CMJ performance reflects the coordinated contribution of multiple joints and muscle groups during an explosive movement.

Consequently, greater knee extensor strength does not necessarily guarantee greater jump height.

Interpreting the different profiles

An athlete in the upper-left quadrant demonstrates relatively high knee extensor strength but comparatively low CMJ performance. This profile may suggest that the athlete possesses a reasonable force-producing capacity but does not express it as effectively during a ballistic movement. Potential contributing factors include movement coordination, countermovement strategy, rate of force development, and the contribution of other muscle groups.

In contrast, an athlete in the lower-right quadrant achieves relatively high CMJ performance despite lower knee extensor torque. Such a player may compensate through effective intermuscular coordination, favorable movement mechanics, or contributions from the hip and ankle extensors. These explanations remain hypotheses that require additional testing.

Practical application

  • Jump force-time characteristics and concentric impulse.
  • Rate of force development and movement strategy.
  • Reactive and ballistic strength qualities.
  • Hip and ankle extensor contributions.

Conversely, an athlete with high jump performance but relatively low knee extensor strength may benefit from further examination of maximum force capacity. Strength capacity and strength expression are related but distinct qualities.

5. CMJ Height and Percentage Body Fat: Understanding Anthropometric Influences

The third quadrant combines CMJ height with percentage body fat (PBF). Unlike the previous comparison, this graph includes a variable for which lower values may be favorable for certain performance tasks, although this relationship should never be interpreted without considering the athlete's health, body composition, and sporting demands.

Vertical jumping requires athletes to accelerate their body mass against gravity. Consequently, body composition can influence the relationship between force-producing capacity and the mass being accelerated. However, body fat percentage alone cannot explain jumping performance.

What does the graph reveal?

The lower-right quadrant contains athletes with relatively high CMJ height and lower body fat percentage. This combination may be favorable for vertical jumping, but it should not be interpreted as evidence that reducing body fat would necessarily improve performance.

The upper-right quadrant includes athletes who achieve relatively high jump heights despite having higher PBF values. These individuals may possess sufficient absolute force and power capabilities to compensate for differences in body composition.

Practical application

Body composition data should be interpreted alongside performance outcomes rather than used as an isolated target. An athlete with low CMJ performance and high PBF should not automatically be prescribed a weight-loss intervention. The practitioner should first consider whether the performance limitation is related to insufficient force production, movement strategy, training history, or other factors.

This is particularly important in female athletes, where nutritional adequacy, energy availability, menstrual health, and long-term athlete well-being must remain central considerations. The quadrant is useful for identifying relationships worth investigating, not for establishing an ideal body fat percentage.

6. CMJ Height and Modified Reactive Strength Index: Two Perspectives on Jump Performance

The fourth quadrant compares CMJ height with the modified reactive strength index (RSI-mod).

RSI-mod = CMJ height ÷ time to take-off

This variable provides information about the relationship between jump displacement and the time required to produce it. An athlete may achieve a relatively high jump by using a longer countermovement strategy, whereas another may reach a similar height in less time. Although both athletes demonstrate comparable jump outcomes, their movement strategies may differ.

What does the quadrant reveal?

Athletes in the upper-right quadrant combine relatively high jump heights with high RSI-mod values. Athletes in the lower-right quadrant demonstrate relatively high CMJ height but lower RSI-mod values, which may indicate that they require more time to produce their jump performance. Conversely, athletes in the upper-left quadrant show comparatively low jump height but relatively high RSI-mod values.

Practical application

Athletes with high CMJ height but relatively low RSI-mod may warrant further analysis of countermovement duration, braking characteristics, and concentric force production. Athletes with low CMJ height but relatively high RSI-mod may benefit from additional evaluation of maximum force capacity and total propulsive impulse.

There is also an important methodological consideration: CMJ height is mathematically included in the calculation of RSI-mod. Consequently, these variables are not statistically independent. This quadrant is most useful for examining how jump height and movement time combine, rather than treating RSI-mod as a completely separate neuromuscular capacity.

7. CMJ Height and Maximal Running Speed: Connecting Gym Performance With Field Performance

The final quadrant compares CMJ height with maximal running speed measured during a 30 m sprint test. This comparison connects a commonly used gym-based performance assessment with a field-based locomotor outcome.

In our published investigation, bilateral CMJ height was positively associated with maximal running speed (r = 0.649). This moderate-to-strong relationship indicates that players who jumped higher generally tended to demonstrate greater running speeds. However, the association was not perfect.

What does the quadrant tell us?

The upper-right quadrant identifies athletes with relatively high CMJ height and maximal running speed. The lower-right quadrant includes athletes with favorable CMJ performance but comparatively low sprint velocity. These athletes may have sufficient vertical power capacity but could be limited by sprint-specific factors such as acceleration mechanics, horizontal force application, or sprint technique.

The upper-left quadrant includes athletes who demonstrate relatively high sprint velocity despite lower CMJ performance. Their sprint performance may reflect effective locomotor mechanics, favorable anthropometric characteristics, or other qualities not fully captured by vertical jump testing.

Practical application

Player A: High CMJ height, low sprint velocity. This athlete may warrant further assessment of horizontal force production, sprint mechanics, acceleration performance, and sprint-specific strength qualities.

Player B: Low CMJ height, high sprint velocity. This athlete may warrant greater attention to vertical force production and lower-body strength, provided these qualities are relevant to their playing demands.

Importantly, neither profile establishes a causal limitation. The quadrant helps identify where more specific testing may be useful.

8. From Visualization to Individualized Training Decisions

The greatest value of performance quadrant analysis is not the classification itself. It is the opportunity to connect assessment results with individualized training decisions.

9. Why Context Matters: Comparing Athletes Across Different Groups

In the presented figures, athletes are differentiated by national-team category: senior (A), U17, and U19. This additional information provides a useful layer of context.

In practice, it may be useful to compare athletes using whole-squad reference values, age-category-specific reference values, playing-position-specific reference values, or individual historical performance values. Each comparison answers a different question.

The most useful reference is not necessarily the team average. It is the reference that best matches the decision being made.

10. Scientific Considerations: What Quadrant Analysis Can and Cannot Tell Us

Although quadrant visualization is intuitive, its simplicity can also create interpretation problems if methodological considerations are overlooked.

  • The position of an athlete depends on the selected reference values.
  • A quadrant does not establish the magnitude or practical significance of a performance difference.
  • Quadrant analysis is descriptive. It does not demonstrate causality or identify a physiological limitation with certainty.
  • Visual relationships should be supported by appropriate statistical analyses when the objective is scientific inference.
  • Variable selection should be guided by physiological relevance rather than simply exploring every possible combination.

For longitudinal athlete monitoring, quadrant changes should also be interpreted against measurement reliability and meaningful-change thresholds. Crossing a reference line does not, by itself, demonstrate a meaningful adaptation.

11. Making Sport Science More Accessible Through R and Shiny

The quadrant graphs presented in this article were developed using R, a programming language widely used for statistical analysis and scientific visualization.

To make this approach more accessible to practitioners, I have also developed an interactive R Shiny application that allows users to explore performance relationships without requiring programming knowledge.

Users can upload datasets, select numerical variables for the horizontal and vertical axes, customize variable titles, and differentiate athletes using categorical characteristics such as team, playing position, or national-team category.

Rather than creating a separate graph manually for every comparison, practitioners can explore different combinations within a single interface. The same visualization framework can therefore be adapted to different performance questions and integrated into broader athlete-monitoring systems.

12. Final Thoughts: Better Decisions, Not Just Better Graphs

Athlete profiling should be more than a collection of isolated performance measurements. A comprehensive assessment provides valuable information about muscular strength, power, contractile characteristics, body composition, and locomotor performance. However, the real challenge is integrating these measurements into a coherent understanding of the athlete.

Performance quadrant analysis offers a simple and practical way to visualize relationships between different physical qualities. It helps identify athletes who demonstrate favorable combinations of performance characteristics, those who possess contrasting strengths, and those who may benefit from further assessment.

Perhaps most importantly, it facilitates communication between sport scientists, strength and conditioning coaches, and technical staff. Nevertheless, quadrant analysis should never be treated as a substitute for rigorous scientific interpretation. Its value lies in supporting the decision-making process, not automating it.

Measure what matters. Visualize what is relevant. Interpret within context. Train according to individual needs.

When these principles are aligned, athlete monitoring becomes more than data collection. It becomes a practical tool for individualized performance development.
